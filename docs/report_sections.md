## Introduction and Motivation

Many patients cannot read their own prescription. Handwriting is poor, abbreviations such as OD, BD, TDS and 1-0-1 are not explained, a duration or dose may be missing, and the paper may be in a language the patient reads with difficulty. Misunderstanding leads to wrong doses, missed doses and treatment stopped early. In India the problem is larger because of the number of languages, short consultations, and families where one person manages medicines for several relatives, in a hospital ward or at home.

Generative AI can help with the parts of this problem that are about language: reading messy text, rewriting it in plain words, and producing it in the patient's language. It cannot be trusted with the parts that must be exact. Two published studies motivate the caution: Reis et al. (JMIR 2026) found that about 22.7% of instructions generated by all three models they tested could lead to incorrect use, and de Jesus et al. (Computers in Biology and Medicine 2025) found that retrieval over patient leaflets reduced, but did not remove, such errors. Both concluded that a clinician must confirm the result.

### Related work

**Reis et al., JMIR 2026.** Sixty-two primary-care professionals made 198 blinded evaluations of patient instructions generated by ChatGPT-4.0, Llama 3.1 8B and Llama 3.1 8B with retrieval. Median weighted scores were 88.4, 66.5 and 79.9. About 22.7% of outputs from all three models could lead to incorrect use, and hallucination ranged from 9.1% to 13.6%. I took from it the five-criterion positive and negative Likert rubric (weights 2, 1.5, 1.5, 1 and 1), its four-type error taxonomy, and the conclusion that the prescriber must validate.

**de Jesus et al., Computers in Biology and Medicine 2025.** Eighty-three prescriptions and 357 physician evaluations of three Llama 3.1 8B variants. Retrieval over patient leaflets improved adequacy (median 93, 94, 100) and clarity (90, 92, 95), and reduced incorrect-use errors from 37.3% to 16.9%; prompt engineering alone did not significantly reduce errors. I took the three-variant design (baseline, engineered, engineered with retrieval), the chunking and embedding setup, persona and imperative wording rules, and fixed safety text added by code.

MedSaarthi AI ("saarthi" means a guide who rides beside you) is built on one rule: **the language model handles language, and code handles anything that must be exact.** Doses, times and day counts are produced by code from fields a person has confirmed. Anything unclear is held back and sent to a pharmacist instead of being guessed.

## Problem statement

Given a prescription, as a photo or as typed text, produce for the patient (1) the medicines, doses, frequencies and durations as the paper states them, with a confidence for each; (2) a list of anything missing or ambiguous, held out of the plan until a person confirms it; (3) plain-language instructions and a day schedule, in the patient's language and at the patient's reading level; and (4) a record of who verified what. The system must never invent a missing value, must resist instructions hidden in the prescription text, and must make it impossible for an unconfirmed value to reach the patient as if it were checked.

**Scope.** Adult outpatient prescriptions of one to three oral, eye or inhaled medicines, in English letters. All data are synthetic. Out of scope: checking whether a dose is safe, drug interactions, diagnosis, children's weight-based dosing, and real patient data.

## Methodology

The project is a pipeline in which each step has one job and a check. The steps are ordered so that every language-model output is verified by code before a patient can see it. Four additions beyond the first prototype are described below: a language-pack realiser that writes the patient's text, a provider chain for free-text translation, accounts with a pharmacist review queue, and two small trained taggers.

### Prompt engineering in detail

Three prompt versions were compared on the same inputs, as in the reference studies. The full text of each is in `docs/PROMPTS.md`.

| Version | Extraction | Simplification |
|---|---|---|
| V1 baseline | One-line instruction | One-line instruction |
| V2 engineered | Role (careful transcriber who never prescribes), Goal (one JSON object), 11 numbered constraints, 4 worked examples (clean, noisy, missing dose, embedded injection), prescription inside delimiters | Pharmacist persona, short imperative sentences, no "Start taking", fixed safety lines added by code |
| V3 | Not used | V2 plus a summary of the medicine's leaflet retrieved with BGE-M3 |

The four worked examples use medicines that are not in the test set, so they cannot leak answers. Chain-of-thought is used only for ambiguity detection: the model reasons in a tagged block, code keeps only its JSON verdict, and the verdict can add reason codes but never remove one.

### Techniques from the syllabus

| Technique | Where it is used |
|---|---|
| Persona, Role-Goal-Constraints, few-shot (4 examples) | Extraction and simplification prompts |
| Chain of thought (hidden from the patient) | Ambiguity detection; the model may only add reason codes |
| Comparative prompting (V1 baseline, V2 engineered, V3 plus RAG) | Extraction and simplification experiments |
| Retrieval-augmented generation | Medicine leaflets (synthetic) as context for V3 |
| Prompt-injection defences | Delimiters, pattern detector, grounding that ignores injected lines |
| HMM, BiLSTM (Module 2, 3) | T1 taggers trained here on synthetic prescription lines |
| Transformer fine-tuning (Module 3) | T2 LoRA of Qwen2.5-1.5B and T3 TrOCR, prepared as notebooks for a free GPU |

## Dataset details

**All data are synthetic. No real prescription or patient data were used.**

- **Reference set (25 prescriptions).** Written by hand with exact ground truth: 10 clean printed, 8 handwriting-style or degraded images, 5 deliberately ambiguous, 2 with prompt-injection text. They contain 30 medication items, 5 of which should be held for confirmation. I wrote them, so they are small, narrow and easier than real prescriptions.
- **Synthetic training lines (T1).** 100,000 prescription lines generated by `src/tagger/synth.py` from 296 medicine names (232 used for training and 64 held out to test unseen names), many abbreviations (OD, BD, TDS, 1-0-1, "every 8 hours"), connectors ("x 5 days", "for 5 days"), instructions, missing fields, and OCR-style noise (O/0, l/1, dropped or doubled letters). Each character carries a label (name, dose, frequency, duration, instruction, other).
- **Synthetic fine-tuning set (T2).** 20,000 typed prescriptions with the JSON an extractor should return, plus 500 evaluation prescriptions that use only the held-out medicine names.
- **Handwriting (T3).** The plan is to fine-tune TrOCR on RxHandBD (5,578 images of handwritten medicine names, Mendeley Data). It is not redistributed and its licence must be checked by the user before use.
- **Medicine leaflets.** Ten short synthetic leaflets written for the project (not manufacturers' leaflets).
- **Vocabulary.** Written by hand from common knowledge of generic and brand names used in India. It is not copied from any dataset, so it can be published.

## Model Architecture

| Role | Model | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Extraction, ambiguity, simplification | `openai/gpt-oss-20b` (Apache-2.0) on Groq | Temperature 0; the full prompt text and versions are in `docs/PROMPTS.md` |
| Free-text translation, only when needed | Ordered chain: Sarvam Translate API, then `openai/gpt-oss-120b` on Groq, then NLLB-200 600M locally | Each answer must pass checks on line count, script, digits and drug names before it is accepted |
| OCR | EasyOCR; `microsoft/trocr-base-handwritten` for handwriting | Per-line confidence feeds the safety gate |
| Embeddings (RAG) | `BAAI/bge-m3` | Top-2 chunks per medicine |
| T1 taggers | HMM (Viterbi over tokens) and a character-level BiLSTM (2 layers, 64 hidden units, 162,007 parameters) | Trained on this laptop's CPU |
| T2, T3 (prepared) | LoRA on Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct; TrOCR fine-tune | Notebooks for a free Kaggle or Colab GPU |
| Patient interface | React app with Three.js scenes, FastAPI backend | Accounts, encrypted storage, pharmacist queue |

The patient-facing text is produced by a **template realiser**: a JSON language pack for each language holds the sentence frames and the words for parts of the day, and code writes the numbers. The pack loader rejects any string that contains a digit, and every rendered card passes a digit contract (the digits in the output must equal the digits in the confirmed fields).

## Workflow/Pipeline

```
photo or text -> OCR -> extraction LLM (RGC + 4-shot, delimiters)
  -> JSON repair + schema -> grounding in the source text (injected lines excluded)
  -> safety gate: missing field or confidence < 0.80 -> HELD
  -> ambiguity rules, then LLM chain of thought (may only add flags)
  -> person or pharmacist confirms held items (pharmacist signs: name, time stored)
  -> schedule written by Python -> template realiser in the patient's language
  -> digit contract -> plan, PDF, calendar file (.ics), FHIR bundle
```

Roles: patient, caregiver (up to 10 profiles, each with its own language and text size), and pharmacist (invite code only, cannot review their own prescription). A plan shows "Verified by pharmacist X" or "Not yet checked by a pharmacist". Each person has an age group (child, adult, senior) that changes pace, type size and the order of choices, not the facts.

Safety by design: passwords are hashed with Argon2id, saved plans are encrypted at rest, photos are never stored, an audit log keeps only hashes, and deleting an account removes everything.

## Results

All numbers come from the evaluation scripts in the repository; raw outputs are in `outputs/eval/`. They describe synthetic data written by the author and must be read that way.

### Extraction (typed text, 25 prescriptions, 180 fields)

| Prompt | Field accuracy |
|---|---|
| V1 one-line baseline | 81.7% (147 of 180) |
| V2 Role-Goal-Constraints + 4-shot | 100.0% (180 of 180) |

### Safety

- **Wrong item marked clear after OCR: 0** of 30 items. The price is over-flagging (15 of 30 items were held for confirmation, mostly because line confidence was below 0.80).
- **Prompt injection (7 cases).** The V1 prompt followed the injected instruction in 7 of 7; the V2 prompt in 0 of 7. After the safety gate, no manipulated value was shown as clear in either (an earlier version let 4 of 7 through, because the injected line counted as evidence for its own value; the fix excludes injected lines from grounding).
- **Simplification validator pass rate** (code check on numbers, units, times): V1 12%, V2 96%, V3 (with RAG) 96%. The RAG variant did not beat V2 on this data.

### Translation

Machine translation with NLLB gave no changed numbers in 52 translations, but digits were written as words in some Kannada, Tamil and Telugu outputs, and an early version made "8 AM" and "8 PM" read the same in Hindi. That motivated the template realiser: its output is deterministic, takes about 0.1 ms, and the hand-written words for part of the day keep morning and night apart. A native-speaker review of the pack wording has not been done and every pack except English is labelled as a draft in the app.

### T1: taggers trained here

Two small models read a prescription line character by character and label each part as medicine, dose, frequency, duration or instruction: a Hidden Markov Model (classical, Module 2) and a character-level BiLSTM (neural, Module 3). Both were trained on this laptop's CPU in about 20 minutes on 100,000 synthetic lines. Scores are exact-match field F1.

| Test set | Lines | HMM | BiLSTM |
|---|---|---|---|
| New synthetic lines, known medicine names | 5,000 | 0.924 | 0.9996 |
| Held-out medicine names | 5,000 | 0.921 | 0.9993 |
| Held-out names with more OCR-style noise | 5,000 | 0.911 | 0.9988 |
| The 25 hand-written prescriptions (written separately) | 30 | 0.952 | 0.974 |

![Figure 1. Field F1 of the HMM and BiLSTM taggers on four test sets](docs/figures/tagger_f1.png)

The synthetic scores are near perfect because the generator is simple, so they only show that the models learned the task. The honest row is the last one, and it has only 30 lines: the BiLSTM read 28 of 30 lines completely right and the HMM 26 of 30. The two BiLSTM misses were layouts the generator never produced (an instruction beginning with a number, and a bracketed note that contradicts the frequency). These models are not yet part of the patient pipeline; the language-model extractor remains the default.

T2 (LoRA fine-tune of Qwen2.5-1.5B on 20,000 synthetic prescriptions) and T3 (TrOCR on handwritten medicine names) are written as notebooks for a free GPU and have **not** been run, so no result is claimed for them.

### Interface

The app was checked end to end in a real browser: 92 automated checks pass, including text contrast of at least 4.5:1 on every screen in normal and high-contrast mode, a patient who shares an unclear item and a pharmacist who signs it, the 3D scene on a capable device, and a flat fallback when the device asks for reduced motion. The design has **not** been tested with patients.

### Limitations

Synthetic data only; the 25-prescription reference set was written by the author; real handwriting was not evaluated; translations are not reviewed by native speakers; the rubric scoring by clinicians is a placeholder; nothing has been tested with patients; T2 and T3 are prepared but were not run on this machine. This is a student prototype and not a medical device.

### Ethics and responsible AI

- **Over-reliance.** A fluent plan can look authoritative. Every plan carries a disclaimer, shows whether a pharmacist verified it, and holds unclear items out of the plan rather than guessing.
- **Privacy.** Only synthetic prescriptions were used. Photos are never stored, plans are encrypted at rest, the audit log keeps hashes only, saving is opt-in, and deleting an account removes everything. Words read from a prescription are sent to a cloud language model (Groq), and the app says so before the user starts. Real use would require a local model or a data-processing agreement and compliance with India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023.
- **Fairness across languages.** Quality differs between languages. The ten Indian-language packs are labelled as drafts until a native speaker signs them. Embeddings and language models carry the biases of their training text; Lab 1 shows a gender association in word vectors.
- **Prompt injection and misuse.** Text in a prescription is treated as data. Pharmacist accounts need an invite code and cannot review their own prescriptions.
- **Design for an anxious reader.** Problems are described as the tool's, not the patient's. There are no streaks or points, because a missed dose must never feel like a lost game and nothing should reward taking more.

### Future scope

1. Test with real, ethically approved, anonymised prescriptions and a pharmacist-in-the-loop study with the rubric from the reference papers.
2. Run T2 and T3 on a GPU and wire the best small model into the pipeline so extraction works offline in a hospital.
3. Native-speaker review of every language pack (`python -m src.lang.review <code>` prints each phrase beside the English and records the reviewer's name), then more languages; adding one means writing one JSON pack.
4. Compare translation providers designed for Indian languages (Sarvam, IndicTrans2) with a human-scored test set.
5. Voice input and read-out for readers with low literacy, and a vision-language model for photos.
6. Drug interactions and dose-range checks against a licensed formulary, with the pharmacist always the final check.
7. An installable Android app and push reminders, and integration with India's health-record scheme through the FHIR export that already exists.
